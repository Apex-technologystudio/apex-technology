"Cheap website" and "bad website" are not the same thing, but they end up the same often enough that people assume they are.

The usual story: a low quote, a site that looks fine on the developer's laptop, and six months later the owner is paying somebody else to start again. The money was not wasted on being cheap. It was wasted on buying the wrong things cheaply.

Here is where a small budget should actually go.

## Spend on these first

**Speed.** Most of your visitors arrive on mobile data. A page that takes six seconds loses people before they see anything, and Google ranks slow pages lower. Speed is not a luxury feature — it is the difference between a visitor and a bounce.

**Mobile layout.** Not "it works on mobile" — genuinely built for a phone held in one hand. In Pakistan the majority of traffic is mobile, and a desktop site squeezed onto a phone reads as an afterthought because it is one.

**An obvious way to contact you.** A phone number and a WhatsApp button that work in one tap. Most small business sites lose enquiries here, not on design.

**Being findable.** Correct page titles, real descriptions, a sitemap, and content that answers what people actually search. This costs almost nothing at build time and is expensive to bolt on later.

## What can wait

- **Custom illustration and animation.** Nice. Not why anyone buys.
- **A large photo shoot.** Start with a handful of real photos of your actual shop or work.
- **Twenty pages.** Five pages that answer real questions beat twenty that repeat each other.
- **A blog you will not write.** An empty blog looks worse than no blog.
- **A login system, dashboard or online payments** — unless your business genuinely needs them on day one.

## The questions that protect your money

Ask these of anyone quoting you, including us:

1. **"Is this a fixed price?"** If the answer involves hourly rates and "it depends", you cannot budget for it.
2. **"What exactly is included?"** Get the page list in writing.
3. **"Who owns the site when it is finished?"** You should. Ask about the domain and hosting accounts too — in your name, not the developer's.
4. **"Can I update the text myself?"** If every small change is a paid job, a cheap site becomes expensive.
5. **"What happens after launch?"** Ask what is covered and for how long.

That third one matters more than people expect. A site you do not control is a site you may have to rebuild to escape.

## Starting small is not the same as starting badly

The right approach on a tight budget is a small site built properly, not a big site built carelessly. Five solid pages — home, what you do, who it is for, prices if you publish them, contact — will out-perform a twenty-page site that loads slowly and says nothing specific.

Built that way, it extends later. Built badly, it gets replaced.
